The challenge

From standardized labels to authentic consumer language

Initial analysis

The first stages cleaned 93,626 credit card complaint records and analyzed standardized Issue/Sub-issue descriptions. The preprocessing reduced word counts by 40.24%, but only 55 unique descriptions remained.

Early classifiers scored 100% because the model inputs included information derived from the target Issue labels: target leakage, not real-world predictive success.

Improved analysis

To strengthen the analysis, the team recovered 28,841 actual consumer narratives from historical CFPB archives. A reproducible sample of 5,000 yielded 4,999 usable narratives.

The new analysis tested sentiment, seven NMF topics, six text clusters, and a held-out issue classifier—without relying on the standardized issue description as predictive text.

Business interpretation

What organizations can learn

Complaint triage and routing

Predictive classification could assist intake teams in routing disputes, reporting concerns, and account problems. With 67.25% accuracy, it should support—not replace—human judgment.

Emerging operational pain points

Topic and cluster patterns highlight disputes, refunds, payments, fraud, and credit reporting as areas for closer review. The results do not establish trends over time.

Interpret sentiment carefully

Positive wording in a complaint does not mean a positive customer experience. Lexicon polarity can miss context, mixed experiences, and the severity of unresolved issues.

Monitor model quality

Company names influenced some topics and clusters. Real deployments would need category-level error review, privacy protections, model monitoring, and periodic validation.

Methodological integrity: Only about 30.8% of the original complaints had usable narratives, and the modeling used a sample. These results are exploratory. They should not be generalized to every consumer or used to make automated decisions about individual complaints.
